## Part B - Survey Evaluation:

Identify and describe survey features:

```
1. Sample type

The first stage of probability sampling was to assign each grouping of telephone numbers (a "record") "to a stratum within its province". The second stage of sampling employed simple random sampling without replacement of records, selected from each stratum (User Guide 9). The third stage of sampling is described as "age order selection", in which the number of eligible persons living in a household is determined, and then these eligible persons in the household are placed in "age order rank". This method was employed for both the telephone and electronic surveys (User Guide 3).

2. Sample size

The target sample size was 20,000, and the final sample size (excluding 'rejected' respondents) was 20,258 (User Guide 11).

3. Target population

"Persons aged 15 years and over living in private households in Canada, excluding residents of the Yukon, Northwest Territories and Nunavut and full time residents of institutions" (quoted from https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/catalogue/45250011).

4. Sampling frame

"The survey frame was created using two different components:
• Lists of telephone numbers in use (both landline and cellular) available to Statistics Canada from various sources (telephone companies, Census of population, etc.);
• The Address Register (AR): List of all dwellings within the ten provinces." 

Approximately 86% of telephone numbers were connected to the AR, and the remaining 14% of unconnected telephone numbers were still included in the frame to ensure a good representation of all households with telephone numbers. Households without a telephone were excluded from the sampling frame. (User Guide 9).

5. Survey mode(s) 

Telephone and electronic surveys with cross-sectional design (User Guide 2-3).

6. Timeline

April 3rd to Dec. 28th, 2018 (User Guide, 2).

7. Response rate

Response rate was 52.8% (User Guide 11).

8. Weights

Standard bootstrap weights "created for the purpose of design-based variance estimation". As the records for the survey frame represent
groups of telephonme numbers, it was necessary for each record within a stratum to have "an equal probability of selection". "This probability is equal to:

Number of records sampled in the stratum
----------------------------------------
Number of records in the stratum from the survey frame" (User Guide 12)

Ultimately, "there were several weight adjustments performed that depended on the province, care giving/receiving status, stratum, age and sex of the respondent" (User Guide 13).

9. Data processing

"Most of the ongoing data processing steps are standard, including consistency edits and family edits.
All social surveys have variable names of 8 characters or less and the following reserve codes:
6 Valid skip
9 Not stated" (User Guide 8). 

10. Cleaning, imputation, etc

Data processing further included a data capture program (entries were entered directly by respondents completing the electronic questionnaire, and by interviewers completing interviews with respondents via telephone); coding of write-in responses, using existing and new categories based on user input; edit and imputation, to remove some responses determined to be "out-of-scope", and to correct "deterministically" some missing or incorrect information; and creation of combined and derived variables, in order to "facilitate analysis" (User Guide 12-13).

11. Sources of error

Sampling and non-sampling errors (User Guide 1). It is also acknowledged that, because the survey methodology has been updated over time, it cannot be definitively determined if "differences in a variable are attributable to an actual change in the population or to changes in the survey methodology" (User Guide 8). In calculating the standard error of the survey's estimates, it is determined that "chances are about 68 out of 100 that the difference between a sample estimate and the true population value would be less than one standard error, about 95 out of 100 that the difference would be less than two standard errors, and virtually certain that the differences would be less than three standard errors" (User Guide 20).

12. Limitations, known biases, etc

One limitation is that approximately 2% of selected respondents were unable to respond due to language barriers, a health condition,
a disability, or "problems related to aging", and so a proxy was permitted to respond on behalf of the respondent. This could compromise
the accuracy of the proxy's response in comparison to what the selected respondent would have responded if they were able (User Guide 3).

Another limitation is that approximately 10.2% answered "yes" to a question indicating that "they have received care during the past 12
months", yet answered no to all further questions clarifying the type of care they had received. As such, these respondents were not
classified as "care receivers"; however, there is no way to clarify why they responded yes to the initial question, or if this was truly
an inaccurate response. The same issue cropped up in 3.5% of respondents who identified themselves as caregivers (User Guide 3-4). These percentages indicate a possible limitation in the original survey design.

A known bias is that the study employed rejective sampling, terminating the interview early if an initial set of questions did not indicate the respondent to be either a caregiver or a care receiver (User Guide 10). This early termination has the possibility of biasing the results if the survey design of these initial questions does not adequately assess if they are a caregiver or care receiver.
